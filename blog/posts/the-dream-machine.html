The Dream Machine

25 Oct 2026 — 5 min read

Your computer might stop running software. It might start imagining it.

In 2024, Google researchers got a neural network to play Doom. There was no game engine, no level files and no code. The model looked at what you had just done, guessed what the screen should show next, and painted it. Tens of times a second, it dreamed the game into existence.

It was crude and expensive, and it fell apart if you looked at it wrong. But it raises a question that gets stranger the longer you sit with it: what if the tower we’ve spent fifty years building is optional?

The tower we take for granted

Every game you’ve played sits on a mountain of machinery. An engine runs on an operating system, which runs on a kernel, which juggles dozens of background processes while a GPU grinds through brutal arithmetic to paint pixels that last a fraction of a second. All of that exists to fake a world that only has to feel right.

So assume the cost problem gets solved, through distilled models, in-memory compute and purpose-built silicon, until a neural frame is cheaper than a rasterized one. What does a computer look like then?

One model, one steady hum

The hardware runs a single enormous model, and its workload barely changes. A quiet menu and a chaotic boss fight cost almost the same, give or take how many neurons fire. There’s no scheduler refereeing between apps, because there are no apps. There are only your intentions and the model’s running guess at what you want to see next.

Software that lives for ten seconds

Say you need a unit converter. The system writes exactly the code that task requires, runs it and throws it away. There’s no settings page, no updater and no login screen, just a tool that exists for as long as you need it and does one job perfectly.

Software stops being something you install and becomes something you summon, like a thought.

Precision becomes a dial

Here’s where it gets tricky. In a game, a blade of grass in the wrong place hurts no one. In a bank ledger, a wrong digit is a disaster.

So the machine needs a tolerance setting: loose and fluid for play, nearly exact for arithmetic. Engineers borrow a mindset from quantum computing, where errors aren’t eliminated but budgeted and corrected. In practice that means error-correcting checks, redundant sampling and verification passes, with the system spending more compute as the stakes rise.

Precision stops being a property of the hardware and becomes something you pay for, moment by moment.

The floor beneath the dream

Even a dreaming machine needs something solid. Your saved game, your files and your account balance can’t live in a model’s fuzzy memory, or the room behind you will quietly rearrange itself when you turn around. Expect a thin layer of exact, deterministic storage and security underneath: far smaller than today’s operating systems, but not zero. The dream needs a floor to stand on.

Where the cloud gets weird

Today, a provider rents you a virtual machine, a private slice of hardware with its own OS and its own idle overhead. In this future, one hosted model serves thousands of people at once, each getting a lightweight, personalised stream. LLM servers already do a version of this, pushing many conversations through a single set of weights. Hosting stops being about carving up hardware and becomes about sharing one imagination across many minds.

The catch

None of this is arriving soon. Precision, persistence and security are real, unsolved problems. And debugging software that is invented fresh every time may be the strangest puzzle of all, because how do you fix a bug that never happens the same way twice?

But the direction is hard to ignore. Computing has spent its whole history getting better at executing. The next chapter may be about predicting.

So someday, the question about your computer may not be “What is it running?”

It may be: “What is it dreaming?”
