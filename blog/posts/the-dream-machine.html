The Dream Machine

25 Oct 2026 — 3 min read

What if your computer stopped running software and started imagining it?

In 2024, Google researchers got a neural network to play Doom. No engine, no level files, no code. The model watched what you did, guessed what the screen should show next, and painted it, frame by frame.

It was crude and expensive. But it made me wonder whether the tower we’ve spent fifty years building is optional.

The tower

Every game sits on a mountain of machinery: an engine, an operating system, a kernel, a swarm of background processes, and a GPU doing brutal arithmetic to paint pixels that last a fraction of a second. All of it exists to fake a world that only has to feel right.

Now suppose a neural frame becomes cheaper than a rasterized one. What does a computer look like then?

One model, one hum

It runs a single large model under a nearly constant load. A quiet menu and a chaotic boss fight cost about the same. There’s no scheduler, because there are no apps, only your intent and the model’s guess at what you want next.

Software that lives for ten seconds

You need a unit converter, so the machine writes exactly that, runs it, and throws it away. No settings page, no updater, no login. Software stops being something you install and becomes something you summon.

Precision as a dial

A misplaced blade of grass hurts no one. A wrong digit in a bank ledger does. So the machine needs a tolerance setting: loose for play, near-exact for arithmetic. Like quantum computing, it wouldn’t eliminate error but budget it, spending more checks and verification as the stakes rise.

The floor

Your saves, files and balances can’t live in fuzzy memory, or the room behind you will quietly rearrange itself when you turn around. Expect a thin layer of exact storage and security underneath, far smaller than today’s operating systems, but not zero. Even a dream needs a floor.

The cloud, reimagined

Today a provider rents you a virtual machine with its own OS and idle overhead. Here, one hosted model serves thousands of people at once, each with a personal stream, much as LLM servers already push many conversations through one set of weights. Hosting becomes sharing one imagination across many minds.

The catch

None of this is close. Precision, persistence and security are unsolved, and debugging software that is invented fresh each time is a puzzle I can’t even picture. But the direction is hard to ignore: less executing, more predicting.

Someday, the question about your computer may not be “What is it running?”

It may be: “What is it dreaming?”
